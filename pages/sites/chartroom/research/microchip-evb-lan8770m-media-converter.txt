===== Candidate use =====

The user is considering the Microchip EVB-LAN8770M_MC development board (EV02N47A; RS stock number 209-7642) to connect a control PC to the ROV over 100BASE-T1 single-pair Ethernet. This is a research candidate only. It has not been purchased, integrated, or tested with the ROV or its tether.

Microchip describes the board as a media converter between 100BASE-TX on an RJ45 connector and 100BASE-T1 on a single-pair interface. Two boards could provide the proposed topology:

<code>
Control PC — 100BASE-TX — surface EVB-LAN8770M_MC — 100BASE-T1 tether pair — ROV EVB-LAN8770M_MC — 100BASE-TX — ROV computer
</code>

This uses each board for one media-conversion endpoint. The user proposes locating one board at the surface and one in the ROV. Compatibility with the ROV computer, including its Ethernet interface and network configuration, has not been established. Both boards also need suitable power at their respective locations.

===== Manufacturer-documented setup =====

The Microchip evaluation board user's guide (document DS50002979A, 2020) states:

  * the board is powered through micro-USB;
  * the RJ45 side uses a Category 5 Ethernet cable and must link at 100 Mb/s, not 10 Mb/s;
  * the single twisted-pair 100BASE-T1 connection uses screw terminal J9;
  * the 100BASE-T1 link requires one end configured as master and the other as slave; on this board, J3 open selects master and J3 closed selects slave;
  * jumpers J6 and J11 must be fitted for normal operation when USB-powered.

These are evaluation-board setup details, not a confirmed installation procedure for the ROV. Confirm the board revisions and guide before wiring. For the proposed two-board arrangement, configure one 100BASE-T1 port as master and the other as slave. The guide's setup uses a single twisted pair; the cited material does not establish suitability of a particular underwater tether, connector, cable length, shielding, or environmental enclosure.

The user proposes short pigtails from each board's T1 screw terminal to a more mechanically robust connector for the inter-board tether link. This keeps the evaluation-board screw terminals at the board end of the pigtails rather than using them as the tether's mechanical termination. The connector type and its electrical, sealing, strain-relief, and mechanical suitability are not yet selected or validated.

===== Assessment and open checks =====

The pair of boards appears to provide a practical conversion path between a control PC and an ROV computer with a conventional 100BASE-TX Ethernet port. Before selecting it, confirm both host interfaces, board power arrangements, the T1 master/slave settings, and the tether pair and connector requirements. Bench-test link establishment and sustained traffic at the intended cable length, and test recovery after disconnects and power cycles. Assess whether the evaluation boards are suitable for their intended surface and ROV environments.

This proposal concerns the Ethernet physical link only. It does not establish network addressing, routing, control-software compatibility, bandwidth under video load, or safe behaviour on link loss. The existing [[sites:chartroom:architecture:communications|communications architecture]] page describes the planned embedded RS-485 bus; this 100BASE-T1 candidate would be a separate Ethernet path and is not an adopted replacement for that bus.

===== Alternative under consideration: simplex optical fibre =====

The user is also considering a single-fibre optical Ethernet link to carry data to and from the ROV. The proposed medium is single-mode fibre, described as 9/125 µm, with bidirectional operation over one strand using wavelength-division multiplexing (WDM), often sold as a BiDi transceiver pair.

In a BiDi arrangement, each end transmits and receives on different wavelengths over the same strand. The two modules must be a complementary pair: for example, one end transmits at 1310 nm and receives at 1550 nm, while the other transmits at 1550 nm and receives at 1310 nm. Matching the physical connector, fibre type, Ethernet speed, wavelength pair, optical reach, and transceiver host interface is essential. Cisco's transceiver guidance describes the wavelength-separated transmit/receive operation; the Smartoptics 1G BiDi datasheet gives the complementary 1310/1550 nm and 1550/1310 nm example.

For the ROV architecture, this would require compatible optical interfaces at the control-PC and ROV ends. If either host lacks an optical Ethernet port, an Ethernet-to-fibre media converter or switch with the correct BiDi optics would be needed at that end. The glass fibre carries data, not electrical power, so vehicle power must be provided separately. A fibre link could avoid a conductive data pair along the tether and offers galvanic isolation, but the cable, terminations, protection, and handling still need to suit tether deployment. Confirm bend radius, tensile and crush protection, water blocking, connector retention, sealing, and pressure/environmental requirements with the cable and connector manufacturers.

This is an alternative physical link to the copper 100BASE-T1 proposal, not a selected design. No fibre cable, BiDi module pair, or Ethernet media converter has been selected or tested. Compare both options using the actual tether length and construction, available endpoint ports, data rate, power budget, connector/strain-relief design, serviceability, and wet/pressure boundaries.

===== User tether requirements and construction leads =====

The user wants the tether to be thin and flexible, physically strong, neutrally buoyant, and difficult to tangle. These are system-level requirements, not yet quantified acceptance criteria. Record the required length and depth, working and peak tension, maximum outside diameter, allowable drag, medium (freshwater or seawater), data and power cores, minimum bend radius, deployment/retrieval method, and expected twist/cycle life before comparing finished tether designs.

Neutral buoyancy does not by itself mean low drag or tangle resistance. Fibron's tether guidance treats diameter, submerged weight, buoyancy, flexibility, tensile strength, current loading, twist, and reel/deployment behaviour as linked design parameters. A small diameter can reduce the area presented to current, while strength members and jacket construction must carry load and protect the data core. The selected buoyancy target should specify freshwater or seawater; it can also be near-neutral or slightly positive/negative to suit the vehicle and operating plan.

Useful construction references, not confirmed product selections:

  * **Blue Robotics Fathom Slim ROV Tether:** The manufacturer lists a 4.0 mm cable with one unshielded twisted pair, Kevlar strength fibres, water blocking, and a polyurethane foam jacket. Its published figures are neutral in freshwater, slightly positive in seawater, 35 kgf working strength, and 155 kgf breaking strength. Blue Robotics says its stiffness is chosen to balance flexibility with reduced tangling. The maker describes it for the primary communications link of a BlueROV2; that does not establish compatibility with this project's Ethernet PHY, 100BASE-T1, or a chosen optical system. Treat it as a mechanical construction reference until the complete electrical link is checked.
  * **Buoyant optical tether:** TEBAOFLEX describes a 5 mm 4-fibre ROV tether with two 9/125 µm single-mode fibres, two multimode fibres, a braided LCP tensile member, foam buoyancy layer, and polyurethane jacket. This is a manufacturer-listed example of a thin, buoyant optical cable, not evidence that its stated construction meets this ROV's working load, depth, abrasion, bend-cycle, or termination needs. Obtain the controlled datasheet and confirm the exact build and test values before considering it.
  * **Custom hybrid construction:** Winchester Interconnect's ROV tether brochure describes custom designs combining power, shielded data pairs, and optical fibres, with aramid reinforcement and buoyancy/jacket options. Fibron describes engineering tether diameter, in-water weight, tensile properties, flexibility, and handling around a specific vehicle and operating profile. These indicate that a custom hybrid tether may be scoped if one cable must carry power and optical data, but no quotation or design has been assessed.

For the anti-tangle requirement, ask suppliers for bending and torsional behaviour, twist/torque balance, minimum dynamic bend radius, abrasion and crush limits, working-load rating, buoyancy at the intended water salinity, and representative deployment/retrieval test evidence. Plan strain relief and a reel or fairlead as part of the system; a tether that is neutrally buoyant may still twist, loop, snag, or drag in current.

===== OpenROV historical implementation: Tenda P200 and HomePlug AV =====

Two primary historical OpenROV references now provide more detail than the repository directory listing alone. They describe different generations/context and should not be collapsed into one hardware revision.

==== OpenROV v2.5 assembly guide (2014) ====

The archived OpenROV v2.5 assembly guide explicitly identifies the **communication PCB from a Tenda P200 HomePlug adapter** as the vehicle-side Ethernet-over-twisted-pair modem. The guide instructs the builder to open the P200 enclosure and separate the Ethernet communication PCB from its power-regulation PCB, retaining the board with the RJ45 connector. It describes plugging that communication PCB into headers on the OpenROV Controller Board and connecting it to the BeagleBone Black Ethernet port with a short Ethernet jumper.

The guide also warns that Tenda changed the P200 PCB between revisions: its July 2013 note says the newer v2 board was laid out as a mirror image of v1 and used female 2×6 headers rather than board stackers. The guide says the newer revision had not yet been worked through in that document. This is a material compatibility warning: do not assume a salvaged P200 communication board has the same orientation, header arrangement, or pinout as the board shown in the guide.

For the topside endpoint, the guide describes using a second HomePlug adapter board in a separate topside enclosure. Its build procedure uses a USB regulator board and custom wiring to power/interface the HomePlug PCB, then brings the tether pair to a two-way terminal block. It states that either tether conductor may be connected to either terminal; the pair has no required polarity for the HomePlug data connection. The laptop connects to the topside adapter through Ethernet, while USB power is applied to the topside assembly. The guide's wiring steps are specific to the described board and revision; they are not a generic wiring diagram for arbitrary HomePlug adapters.

This assembly guide is an archived secondary-hosted copy of OpenROV's 2014 Dozuki instructions. It is useful historical build evidence, but the underlying OpenROV schematic and bill of materials have not yet been traced component-by-component in this review.

==== OpenROV presentation (2017) — reported link performance ====

Charles Cross's *Bridging the Gap Between Industrial and Consumer IoT With DDS* presentation, delivered under the OpenROV heading, contains a slide titled **“Communication Solution: Homeplug AV”**. It states that OpenROV used HomePlug AV over twisted pair and reports:

  * approximately **80 Mbit/s at the MAC layer**;
  * operation as an Ethernet bridge;
  * only two tether wires required;
  * a tested range of approximately **350 m**;
  * an Atheros power-line-communications chipset, packaged in a PCI Express form factor.

These are figures reported in the 2017 presentation, not a guaranteed performance specification for the Tenda P200, the 2.5 assembly, the 2.6 topside board, or the user's own adapters. The presentation does not provide a test method, cable construction, noise environment, packet-loss result, application throughput, or latency distribution on that slide. In particular, the approximately 80 Mbit/s figure is explicitly MAC-layer bandwidth and must not be represented as measured application payload throughput.

The presentation's Atheros/PCI Express description should not be assumed to identify the Tenda P200 communication PCB used in the 2014 v2.5 build guide. They are separate source observations from different dates and may describe different OpenROV hardware iterations.

==== Tenda P200 product specification context ====

Tenda's P200 user guide describes the complete retail adapter as a HomePlug AV device rated at a nominal 200 Mbit/s PLC link rate, with a 10/100 Mbit/s RJ45 interface and a stated power-line transmission distance of up to 300 m. It specifies AC 100–240 V input for the complete retail product. These are manufacturer product ratings for the intact mains-powered adapter, not the OpenROV tether's measured throughput or range. The 200 Mbit/s PHY/PLC rate, OpenROV's 80 Mbit/s MAC-layer figure, and application payload throughput are different measures and must not be compared as if equivalent.

OpenROV's v2.5 guide removes the P200 communication PCB from its original mains power-regulation PCB and provides a separate, board-level power/interface arrangement. This does **not** mean an intact Tenda P200 adapter can be powered directly from a low-voltage ROV supply, nor does it establish that arbitrary DC can safely be injected onto the tether pair. The precise coupling, filtering, isolation, and power-injection circuit must be checked for the exact board revision before combining DC power and HomePlug signalling on shared conductors.

==== Relevance to this ROV investigation ====

The useful architectural precedent is a pair of HomePlug endpoints forming an Ethernet bridge over a two-conductor tether, with one endpoint inside the vehicle and a separately packaged topside endpoint. It supports keeping the vehicle computer's interface as ordinary Ethernet while the tether modem handles the physical transport.

For our investigation, the OpenROV implementation is a reference architecture, not a ready-to-copy wiring design. The user's existing powerline adapters and the APISQUEEN module remain distinct hardware candidates; neither has been identified as the Tenda P200 revision used by OpenROV. Before bench connection, identify each modem and board revision, determine its DC input and line-coupling arrangement, and establish whether the tether pair carries data only or data plus DC. Then test link establishment, sustained application throughput, latency/jitter, packet loss, and recovery while operating the propulsion electronics. The 2017 350 m result is a useful historical range reference, not an acceptance criterion for our tether.

==== How HomePlug / power-line communication uses a two-wire tether ====

HomePlug AV, based on IEEE 1901 physical-layer and media-access methods, carries network data as a high-frequency modulated signal over a conductor pair. The Ethernet-facing adapter takes Ethernet frames and its power-line modem encodes them across many narrow frequency subcarriers using orthogonal frequency-division multiplexing (OFDM). The receiving modem decodes the signal and returns the frames to Ethernet. Both directions share the same pair under the protocol's channel-access control; the two tether wires are not separate Ethernet transmit and receive wires. The modem can adapt transmission across subcarriers to suit channel conditions. The advertised physical or link rate is not the same as application throughput.

In the OpenROV arrangement, a HomePlug adapter at each end provides the Ethernet bridge, while its line interface couples the PLC signal to the two tether conductors. The OpenROV 2.6 assembly-guide text describes the topside unit as converting the two-wire tether back to Ethernet and identifies a HomePlug board in the assembly. “Powerline” names the technology family; it does not mean the tether must carry mains power. Low-voltage direct current (DC) can share the conductors only when the adapter's coupling and power-injection circuit is designed for that arrangement. The GitHub schematic and bill of materials were not readable through the web reader during this review, so this functional description does not verify their component-level circuit. Do not connect a HomePlug adapter to a tether carrying DC until the exact adapter input, isolation, and coupling arrangement are confirmed.

Before drawing conclusions, record the adapter models and test conditions, including the umbilical conductors and cable length, how power is supplied at each end, link establishment, sustained throughput, packet loss/latency, and recovery after power cycling or interruptions. Confirm that the adapter's electrical ratings and isolation suit the actual ROV power architecture before connecting it to the vehicle. Do not treat a successful short bench link as evidence that the complete tether will work under load, at depth, or during vehicle operation.

===== More integrated converter options =====

Several enclosed products are available as alternatives to open evaluation boards. Their product descriptions target automotive, industrial, or development use; an enclosure alone does not make a converter suitable for exposure to water.

  * **PEAK-System PAE-Media Converter (IPEH-101101):** The manufacturer's manual version 1.1.1 (2026-11-18) specifies an aluminium-profile enclosure, RJ45 for 100BASE-TX/1000BASE-T, H-MTD and MATEnet T1 connectors, configurable operation at 100 or 1000 Mb/s, and 6–36 V DC or USB-C power. The stated ingress protection is IP20. Its connectors are a more integrated option than screw terminals and pigtails, but IP20 means the unit needs a dry, protected location; it is not a wet or pressure-rated enclosure. The product is described for automotive development and test environments.
  * **Datron Technology TE-1000 100BASE-T1 BCM:** The UK supplier describes bidirectional 100BASE-T1/100BASE-TX conversion in a powder-coated galvanised-steel housing, with DIP-switch configuration for master/slave. The reviewed product page does not state an ingress-protection rating or substantiate underwater suitability; request the full specifications before considering installation.
  * **I-NOVATIVE Factory-Link-SPE-M:** The manufacturer's manual specifies an aluminium housing, an IEC 63171-6 T1 industrial jack, RJ45, 5–28 V input, and a −40 to +65 °C operating range. However, the same manual explicitly says it is for development and testing and is not intended for series production. The reviewed manual does not establish a water-ingress rating.

For this ROV, check that any candidate fits inside a dry, pressure-protected compartment or a separately qualified enclosure. Also confirm operating temperature, power input, T1 connector mating parts, cable construction, master/slave operation, availability, and whether the manufacturer supports the intended continuous use. None of these listed units is documented here as submersible or pressure-rated.
