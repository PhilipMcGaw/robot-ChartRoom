This page records a group of user-supplied project and tutorial links. These are research references, not project decisions or validated designs. Summaries below describe what the linked source reports; they do not establish independent testing.

===== Underwater robots and robotic fish =====

  * [[sites:chartroom:research:elementary-auv-rov-education|Elementary AUV/ROV education]] is the related education note. New Atlas's 2010 article, [[https://newatlas.com/book-teaches-kids-to-build-rovs/17371/|Build Your Own Underwater Robot]], reports on Harry Bohm and Vickie Jensen's book, which covers SeaPerch and SeaFox educational remotely operated vehicles (ROVs). The article's photographs show home-built SeaFox examples with a manipulator arm and a custom camera housing. This is a secondary report about a book, not a substitute for the book's instructions.
  * The Arduino Blog's [[https://blog.arduino.cc/2016/09/29/robotic-fish-swims-under-Arduino-control/|robotic fish project]] describes a fish-shaped vehicle made from bent polyvinyl chloride (PVC) pipe, with a tail for propulsion, pectoral fins for steering, and a ballast tank. Its summary identifies an Arduino Pro Mini, a motor-driver board, and a two-cell lithium-polymer battery. The article notes that the television-remote control needs relatively clear water. These are source descriptions; the article does not provide independent performance validation.
  * Open Electronics' [[https://www.open-electronics.org/robofish-create-your-robot-fish-with-arduino/|Robofish]] describes a polystyrene body with three servo-driven body joints, two infrared obstacle sensors, Arduino-compatible control, and a reed-switch activation method. The article includes firmware and a build description. Its sensor-in-a-plastic-bag method is a reported prototype detail, not a pressure-rated or independently verified waterproofing method.
  * Hackaday's [[https://hackaday.com/2021/01/18/robotic-fish-swarm-together-using-cameras-and-leds/|report on Harvard's BlueSwarm robotic fish]] describes decentralised group behaviour using blue LEDs and cameras to estimate neighbouring fish positions. The report identifies four oscillating fins, Raspberry Pi Zero W computers, and Raspberry Pi camera modules. It also notes that visible-light communication depends on water clarity. See the [[https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/scirobotics.abd8668|linked research paper]] for primary research; the Hackaday article is secondary reporting.

===== Control and drawing robots =====

  * Jason Bowling's [[https://medium.com/swlh/building-a-network-controlled-robot-with-arduino-and-raspberry-pi-77dd9134e48c|network-controlled Arduino and Raspberry Pi robot]], published 2020-06-03, is a member-only Medium article. Its accessible introduction states goals of low-latency video, network gamepad control, battery telemetry, and later autonomy and machine vision. The full implementation details were not available during review, so no architecture or performance result is inferred here.
  * Hackster.io's [[https://www.hackster.io/RemoteXY/robotcar-controlled-using-g-sensor-smartphone-9f7eec|Robotcar Controlled Using G-Sensor Smartphone]], published 2016-05-03, describes an Arduino Uno, HC-05/HC-06 Bluetooth module, L298N motor driver, and RemoteXY mobile interface. The phone's motion sensor controls a two-wheel robot. The page supplies example code and a schematic, but this is an educational demonstration and is not evidence of a safety-rated control link.
  * The supplied [[https://www.instructables.com/Arduino-Drawing-Robot/|Arduino Drawing Robot]] page was not readable in the review interface. A separate Arduino drawing-robot resource is already listed in [[sites:chartroom:research:ultrasonic-robotics-and-open-hardware-batch|Ultrasonic robotics and open hardware]]; it is a different URL, and the relationship between the two projects is unconfirmed.

===== Electronics references =====

  * Lewis Loflin's [[https://www.bristolwatch.com/TC4420/TC4420hb.htm|TC4420 H-bridge article]] presents a brushed-motor bridge using four MOSFETs and two TC4420 gate drivers. It names IRFZ44N and IRF4905 devices and states an 18 V maximum for the described design. This is a hobby circuit article, not a manufacturer design review or validated motor controller. Check the TC4420 and MOSFET data sheets, switching behaviour, thermal limits, and protection before using any circuit; the page's operational-mode wording should also be checked against its schematic.
  * Dyethin's [[https://www.dyethin.com/blog/projects/how-to-design-an-overvoltage-protection-circuit|overvoltage-protection tutorial]], dated 2023-09-21, surveys Zener-based circuits, a silicon-controlled-rectifier (SCR) crowbar, and voltage clamping. It includes example circuits and simulation claims. It is a secondary tutorial with inconsistencies in its example descriptions; use manufacturer data sheets and a design review for any real protection circuit, rather than copying the examples as validated designs.
  * Adafruit's [[https://learn.adafruit.com/improve-brushed-dc-motor-performance|Improve Brushed DC Motor Performance]] guide, published 2021-04-11 and last edited 2024-05-15, compares pulse-width modulation (PWM) frequency and current-decay mode for brushed direct-current (DC) motors. It recommends testing slow-decay operation and treats 50–100 Hz as a rule of thumb for many small brushed motors, while noting that low frequencies can cause motor or gearbox vibration and that fast-decay mode may use less power in some battery-powered applications. The guide reports measurements from a set of workshop motors using a motor tester that recorded speed, current, and torque; its results and settings are not universal. For project use, test the selected motor with its normal load and supply, and keep brushed-motor findings separate from brushless thruster control.
  * The supplied [[https://www.instructables.com/Make-Your-Own-ESC/|Make Your Own ESC]] Instructable resolves to a five-step page, but its step text was not readable on 2026-11-08. The linked GreatScott! videos, [[https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=W9IHEqlGG1s|Part 1]] and [[https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=NXkLydhRvS0|Part 2]], describe a do-it-yourself electronic speed controller (ESC) for a brushless direct-current (BLDC) motor using an Arduino Nano and an L6234 motor-driver integrated circuit; [[https://www.hackster.io/news/build-your-own-esc-for-bldc-motors-e608f9ff8056|Hackster's 2017 overview]] reports the same high-level parts. These sources make it a useful educational reference for motor-control concepts, but they do not establish current ratings, robustness, or suitability for a project thruster. Review the schematic, component data sheets, protections, and tested operating envelope before considering any implementation.
  * The supplied [[https://learn.adafruit.com/16-channel-pwm-servo-driver?view=all|Adafruit 16-channel PWM/servo-driver guide]] is already recorded in [[sites:chartroom:research:pca9685|PCA9685 PWM/servo driver research notes]].
