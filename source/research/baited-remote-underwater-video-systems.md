# Baited remote underwater video systems (BRUVS)

## Scope

This page records reference information from the supplied *Rapid Assessment Toolkit | Sharks and Rays*, Tool 4, “Baited Remote Underwater Video Systems (BRUVS)”. It is relevant to underwater observation, biodiversity surveys, and possible future inspection or conservation payloads. It is not a project requirement, survey protocol, or validation of a Chartroom system.

## What BRUVS provide

BRUVS combine a weighted frame, bait arm and bait bag, underwater video camera, and surface float. The unit is deployed to the seafloor, left for a defined period, and recovered for video analysis. The bait attracts animals into the camera's field of view without putting observers in the water.

The method can provide:

- species presence;
- relative abundance between comparable deployments;
- habitat observations; and
- permanent visual records.

Stereo BRUVS use paired cameras and can support length measurement, but require camera rigidity, calibration, and suitable analysis software.

## Survey design and standardisation

Sampling design should follow the study question. Comparisons between fished and unfished areas require comparable deployments in each area; habitat studies require representative deployments across the available habitat types. Habitat type and water visibility should be recorded because they can affect the observed community and the quality of the footage.

The supplied reference identifies the following factors for standardisation:

- deployment time;
- bait amount and preparation;
- depth range;
- frame size and height above the substrate;
- camera and housing arrangement; and
- site and deployment labels.

Typical deployments are described as 60–90 minutes on the seafloor, with approximately 1 kg of bait. A minimum separation of 500 m is given as an example from the Global FinPrint project, not as a universal requirement. The boat should be kept away from the unit after deployment to reduce possible behavioural effects from boat noise; the supplied reference gives 200 m as guidance.

## Hardware and deployment considerations

Frames may use stainless steel, rebar, PVC, acrylic, timber, or other suitable materials. They need enough weight and stability for the sea state and current, attachment points for the recovery line and surface float, and a secure camera housing. The bait arm is commonly 1–1.5 m long.

Camera selection must provide footage clear enough for species identification. The supplied reference gives 1080p at 30 frames per second as a practical example and notes that 90 minutes at that setting may require 20–25 GB of storage. These values are source-specific planning figures, not requirements for a future Chartroom system.

Stereo systems need tightly fixed, paired cameras and a calibration procedure. Each stereo arrangement has its own calibration, which depends on preserving the camera positions and frame setup between deployments.

Deployment planning should account for vessel operations, rope length, depth, current, recovery loads, batteries, memory cards, chargers, and backup storage. Deployments deeper than 50 m may require a heavier frame, lighting, and a winch according to the supplied reference.

## Video analysis

The reference describes a MaxN workflow:

1. Record habitat type and estimated visibility.
2. Pause when a shark or ray is observed.
3. Identify the animal to the lowest reliable taxonomic level.
4. Record the maximum number of individuals of each species visible at one time.
5. Preserve backup copies of each video.

MaxN is a conservative relative-abundance measure. It does not count animals that are never simultaneously visible in the frame and must not be treated as a direct population estimate without an appropriate study design.

Stereo analysis requires purpose-built measurement software and calibrated footage. Video files may also need stitching before analysis, depending on the camera system.

## Relevance to Chartroom

BRUVS is a useful reference for separating:

- a physical observation payload from vehicle safety and control;
- deployment and recovery procedures from video interpretation;
- raw footage retention from derived species or abundance results; and
- a repeatable survey method from a general-purpose underwater camera.

For any future project use, the survey design, camera system, bait handling, animal-welfare and environmental permissions, storage plan, analysis method, and validation criteria would need to be defined separately. A successful recording would establish that footage was captured; it would not by itself establish species-identification accuracy or ecological conclusions.
